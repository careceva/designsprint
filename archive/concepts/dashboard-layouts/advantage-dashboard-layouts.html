Procurement portal · dashboard exploration

Five ways to hold the same numbers.

Five structural layouts for the SME owner's dashboard — each carrying the identical content set, each taking a genuinely different position on how spend is visualised and how you travel from the macro savings figure to the micro act of activating one supplier agreement.

Present in all five

  • 1Total realised savings — hero metric
  • 2Savings by category
  • 3Active vs. available agreements
  • 4Recommended / missed savings
  • 5Path into category browsing
01

The Vault

A vertical narrative with no sidebar at all. The savings figure is treated like a product hero — one number, enormous, alone on white — and everything else is discovered by scrolling down through it.

No chrome competing with the number. Nav is text-only, top-centred; depth comes from scroll.

Structure

  1. Header — translucent, 46px, logo left, five plain text nav links centred, avatar right. No search field, no icons.
  2. Hero — full-bleed white, the savings figure at ~62px/700 weight, a single verification line, two pill buttons.
  3. Category shelf — horizontally scrolling row of tiles, each a category with its savings figure. Bleeds off the right edge deliberately.
  4. Missed savings band — full-width tinted band, editorial two-column: claim + one CTA.
  5. Agreement ledger — below the fold: active vs. available as two stacked lists.

Visual flow & why it works

This is the Apple product-page rhetoric applied to a dashboard: one claim per screen, arrived at in sequence. The eye has nowhere to go but the number, because there is literally nothing else above the fold — no sidebar, no widgets, no chart.

Macro to micro is the scroll itself. You descend from the total, to the categories that compose it, to the specific gap, to the agreement list. Each band answers the question the previous one provokes.

The shelf bleeding off-screen is a deliberate Apple TV borrowing: it signals abundance (“there is more catalogue than fits”) without a count badge.

Data visualisation

Almost none, on purpose. Typography carries the data: one giant figure, per-category figures on tiles, and a delta arrow. The only graphical mark is an optional hairline sparkline under the hero.

Navigation model

Text links + horizontal shelves. Clicking a tile is a full page transition to the category — no drawers, no split panes. Back is browser-back.

Risk: returning daily users must scroll past the marketing-feeling hero every visit. Mitigate by compacting the hero to a 96px band on second visit.

02

The Bento Grid

A modular grid of unequal tiles, each a self-contained answer and a doorway — the widget stack logic of iPadOS, where the largest tile is simply the most important one.

Unequal tiles on a 3-column grid. Size is the only hierarchy device — no colour ranking.

Structure

  1. Header — 46px, breadcrumb left, notification bell + avatar right.
  2. Icon rail — 56px, glyphs only, labels on hover. Buys back ~180px of grid width.
  3. Grid — 3 columns × n rows, 14px gutters. Hero savings tile spans 2×1; category donut 1×1; agreements coverage 1×1; recommended savings 3×1 full-bleed row.
  4. Rows below — further 1×1 tiles: bonus, benchmark, expiring soon. Reorderable.

Visual flow & why it works

The eye lands top-left on the largest tile — Fitts + Gestalt working together: biggest area, most padding, most contrast in its number. It then reads rightward across the two smaller tiles, which are deliberately the same size as each other so neither wins.

The Apple lineage is the widget: each tile is a glanceable summary that is also a tappable doorway. Nothing needs a “view more” link, because the whole tile is the link.

Macro to micro is a zoom: tap the category donut and it expands into the category grid; tap a category and it expands into agreements. Same grammar at every depth.

Data visualisation

Diverse but each mark is small and singular: a sparkline in the hero, a single-arc donut for concentration, a two-segment bar for active vs. available. One mark per tile — never two.

Navigation model

Icon rail for sections, tiles for drill-down, zoom transitions between depths.

Risk: the most familiar dashboard pattern here, so it is the least distinctive — and bento grids rot as tiles accumulate. Cap it at seven tiles and make the eighth a deliberate design decision.

Data lives in the list rows as inline bars — no chart panel anywhere on the screen.

Structure

  1. Header — 46px, centred search field (⌘K), avatar right.
  2. Sidebar — 168px, labelled sections, one hairline divider grouping primary from secondary.
  3. List column — 236px, pinned summary block at top (the hero metric, compact), then every category as a row: name, savings figure, and a two-segment coverage bar.
  4. Detail pane — fluid, shows the selected category: diagnosis, agreement cards, current suppliers.

Visual flow & why it works

Left-to-right reading order maps exactly onto increasing specificity: where am I, what are the parts, what is this part. The eye never jumps back.

The Apple reference is Mail and Finder column view — persistent selection, no page loads, arrow keys move down the list while the right pane keeps pace. It feels like an application rather than a website, which is the correct register for a tool opened weekly.

Macro to micro happens without navigation. Comparing two categories costs two keystrokes, which is the cheapest comparison of any layout here.

Data visualisation

Inline, in-row, tiny. A two-segment bar per row (on-agreement vs. off) and a figure. Denser than any other option and the only one where you can compare all fourteen categories in a single glance without a chart.

Navigation model

Persistent three-pane selection; keyboard-first (↑↓ to move, ↵ to open the agreement page).

Risk: the least emotional of the five. The hero metric is demoted to a sidebar block, which undersells the product's core promise on first visit. Best for the returning power user, weakest at first impression.

04

The Focus Card

An anti-dashboard. One narrow centred column, one decision at a time, paged like a deck of cards — the logic of Apple Fitness rings: a single closable goal, not a report.

+kr 118K / year
You buy kr 620K of office supplies a year, 78% of it at list price. Member terms are 19% below what you paid last quarter.
Skip

One column, ~520px. The next card peeks below at reduced scale, so the queue is felt, not listed.

Structure

  1. Header — a single centred segmented control as the entire navigation: Overview · Categories · Agreements · Savings. No sidebar at any breakpoint.
  2. Compact hero — a progress ring (share of spend on agreement) beside the savings figure. The ring is the chart; the figure is the headline.
  3. The focus card — one recommendation at full attention: supplier, the figure, the reasoning, and a commitment ladder of actions.
  4. Pagination dots + peeking card — six decisions, one visible.
  5. Category grid — reached via the segmented control, not shown here.

Visual flow & why it works

Attention is allocated, not competed for. There is exactly one hero element, and everything on screen is subordinate to it — the purest reading of the Apple principle that a design should have one job per view.

This layout takes the strongest position on the user: an SME owner has ten minutes a week and does not want a report, they want six things to be done with. The dots turn the work into a finite, closable set — the Fitness-ring trick of making progress feel completable.

Macro to micro is collapsed into one screen: the ring is the macro, the card is already the micro. The agreement page is one tap deeper for terms and how-to-buy.

Data visualisation

Deliberately impoverished: one progress ring, one figure per card. There is no category chart on the default view at all — category breakdown lives one tab across. That is the trade this layout makes on purpose.

Navigation model

Segmented control for breadth; card pagination (swipe, arrow keys, J/K) for depth.

Risk: it withholds the overview. A user who wants to browse rather than decide will feel managed rather than served — and the “Savings by category” requirement is satisfied only on a second tab, which some stakeholders will read as non-compliance.

Structure

  1. Header — 46px with a centred segmented control switching the mosaic metric: Spend / Savings.
  2. Summary line — realised against total possible, one line, no card.
  3. Mosaic — the whole main area. Tiles sized by annual spend, each filled left-to-right by coverage percentage; the remaining pale area is literally the gap. Selected tile gets a 2px accent outline.
  4. Inspector — 214px right panel, contextual to selection: the gap figure, the agreements in that category, one primary action. Never a drawer — always present.

Visual flow & why it works

This is the only layout where the answer arrives before reading. Large-and-pale means “big spend, badly covered”, so the eye finds the opportunity by shape and value alone. Office supplies wins attention not because we ranked it but because it looks emptiest.

The Apple lineage is direct manipulation plus the Keynote/Pages inspector: the canvas is the content, the right panel is always about whatever is selected, and selection never navigates away.

Macro to micro is spatial: the mosaic is the estate, clicking a tile fills the inspector, and clicking an agreement in the inspector opens its page. Optionally the tile itself zooms into a sub-mosaic of its agreements.

Data visualisation

A treemap with an embedded part-to-whole fill — two variables (magnitude, coverage) in one mark. The most information-dense visual of the five, and the most distinctive.

Navigation model

Spatial selection with a persistent inspector; optional zoom into a category's sub-mosaic.

Risk: treemaps have a learning curve, and the encoding must be taught in one line (“tile size = spend, fill = on agreement”). With fourteen categories it sings; with forty it becomes confetti. Needs a table view as an accessible equivalent, not an afterthought.

Which one, and why

The five are not five styles of the same idea — they take different positions on what the user came to do. That is the real choice in front of you, and it should be settled with users rather than in a review.

LayoutPosition it takesStrongest for
The VaultThe number is the product; awe first, detail laterFirst login, sales demo, renewal moment
The Bento GridEverything glanceable, every tile a doorBroad weekly check-in; safest default
The LedgerThis is a tool, not a reportReturning power user; comparing categories
The Focus CardYou came to decide, not to browseTen-minute repeat visits; conversion
The AtlasSee the whole estate; the gap is a shapeDiagnosis, advisor-led review, board show-and-tell

My recommendation: prototype 04 and 05 as the pair. They sit at opposite ends — maximum focus versus maximum overview — so testing them together tells you which instinct your users actually have. The Bento Grid is the safe fallback if the answer is “both, a bit”. And whichever wins, steal the Ledger's inline row bars: fourteen categories comparable at a glance, with no chart, is the single most efficient element in this set.

Two notes on the brief

Palette divergence. These wireframes use the Apple-reference palette you specified — #0071E3, #1D1D1F, #86868B, #F5F5F7. Your actual Advantage design system resolves to a different blue (#1b64dc), foreground #0f0f0f, muted #707070, background #fafafa, with radii of 16/24px and Rethink Sans + DM Sans rather than SF Pro. The layouts are palette-independent, but before any of this is built it is worth deciding deliberately whether we are matching Apple's literal values or applying Apple's method to your own tokens. My advice is the latter — the tokens are already good, and a second blue in the codebase will cost you more than it buys.

Supplier logos. The brief names Circle K and Scandic; the asset folder actually holds Q8, Lyreco, Hilti, Ahlsell, Stark and Bygma. Wireframes here use neutral placeholder marks, and any high-fidelity pass should use the logos that exist plus initials chips at identical geometry for suppliers without one — mixed logo shapes are the fastest way to make a clean grid look untidy.
