Product Discovery · Sprint 2 · Working document

Pain map & interview kit

Eight candidate pains reconstructed from what Sweden and Norway actually said, and two interview instruments to find out which are real — one for customers, one for salespeople. Nothing here is a finding yet. It's a list of things to try to disprove.

Known constraint · AI is decided

The AI direction is settled at C level. That's not a reason to skip this research — it's a reason to change its question. We are no longer asking whether AI, we're asking where AI earns its place: which pain is severe enough that an automated intervention is welcome rather than another thing to dismiss. A sprint that picks the wrong pain will build AI nobody uses, and that fails just as expensively as building the wrong thing. See what this changes.

Diagnosis

Two markets, two exercises, and not one sentence about a person struggling

The Norwegian frame holds roughly 150 raw items condensed to 20 key features across 8 categories. Sweden's affinity map holds 28 features across 7 themes. Both are careful, both are well organised, and both answer the same question: what could we build?

Read either one looking for a moment where somebody wanted to do something and couldn't, and you'll find nothing. "Live Agreement Usage Dashboard" is a solution. "Netflix-style layout" is a solution. Even the sharpest item on either board — Sweden's "Active agreements without spend — contact buyer option" — states the solution and only implies the problem underneath it, which is the interesting part: customers are signing agreements they then never buy from.

That's not a failure of either exercise. Feature lists are what you get when you ask people what they want, and both rooms did what was asked. But it does mean the pain map can't be extracted from these boards. It has to be reconstructed — by taking each cluster of feature requests and asking what would have to be true about someone's day for them to ask for that. Then going and checking.

Strip this out before Day 1

Norwegian category 6 (Admin/Editor Tools) and parts of category 5 — drag-and-drop content management, SEO and meta editing, supplier portal access, customer group management, search configuration — are our needs, not customers'. That's roughly 12 items of internal tooling sitting inside what's being treated as market feedback. They're legitimate work; they just aren't pains, and if they stay on the board someone will vote for them on Day 2. Move them to a separate parking list with a name on it.

Before the pains

There are three different people in these lists, and they're being treated as one

Almost every contradiction between the two boards dissolves once you separate the actors. "Show me benchmarking against similar companies" and "let me reorder the same box of gloves in ten seconds" are not competing priorities — they're two different people who have never met.

The Signer

Owner, CFO, finance lead. Bought the service. Will decide whether to keep paying for it.

Wants proof. Every savings dashboard, benchmark, cashback overview and "estimated savings" feature on both boards is written for this person — and they may log in twice a year.

The Buyer

Office manager, site foreman, workshop lead, the owner's partner. Orders the actual things. Often never asked for any of this.

Wants the thing to arrive, today, without a new login. Almost nothing on either board is written for them, which is suspicious given they're the only ones who can move spend onto agreements.

Us

Editors, advisors, sales, support inside Visma.

Wants tooling. Present throughout the Norwegian list. Belongs on a roadmap, not on a pain map.

The single most useful thing you can establish in the interviews: in this company, are the Signer and the Buyer the same person? In a five-person firm they usually are. At forty people they never are — and the gap between them may be the whole problem.

The pain map

Eight candidate pains, ranked by how much evidence actually exists

Each one is stated in the user's voice, tagged with who feels it, and paired with the question that could kill it. Evidence ratings describe how strongly the two boards point at it — not how true it is. Watch for the danger pattern: thin evidence with many proposed features means a lot of people have solved a problem nobody has confirmed.

P1

"We joined, and then nothing really changed."

Who · Signer → Buyer handoff Markets · SE + NO Evidence: strong

Behind: Active vs. Inactive Agreements (NO), Active agreements without spend — contact buyer option (SE), Agreement Activation (NO 8), Quick path from potential to activation (SE 7), Integrated Agreement Activation, onboarding walk-throughs, both markets' notification wishes.

ProbeLook up which agreements sit unused before the call, then open a story with it: "I saw you set up the workwear one back in March — what was going on around then?" If you have no data, ask for the shape rather than the inventory: "Was there something you set up here that just didn't carry on?" Then, separately: "Who else in the company orders things — do they know this exists?"

P2

"I can't prove to anyone that this is worth the money."

Who · Signer Markets · SE + NO Evidence: strong

Behind: the entire Dashboard & Savings category (NO 1) and Savings Intelligence theme (SE 2) — realised savings, savings calculator, cashback per supplier, peer comparison, benchmarking against similar companies, estimated savings prompts. The most heavily requested area on both boards by a wide margin.

Probe"Has anyone ever asked you to justify this subscription? What did you show them?" and "How would you know if you saved money last year — where would you look first?" If they hesitate, that hesitation is the finding.

P3

"I don't know what we've got, so I order from who I always order from."

Who · Buyer Markets · SE + NO Evidence: strong

Behind: Agreement Discovery & Comparison, Category Navigation & Smart Filtering, Recommendations & Popular Agreements, favourites, Netflix-style homepage (NO); Recommended suppliers and categories, Simple customer journey, Within a few clicks you are on top of everything (SE).

ProbeNever ask them to audit their own ordering — they can't, and the attempt turns the interview into a quiz. Ask for the last new supplier instead: "The last time you ordered from somewhere you hadn't used before — how did you end up choosing them?" Whether an agreement existed for it is your homework, done afterwards against their spend data. The finding is the gap between the two, and only one side of it comes from the interview.

P4

"I'd rather talk to a person than click through a system."

Who · Buyer + Signer Markets · SE + NO Evidence: medium

Behind: Advisor Chat & Contact Tools, Supplier Messaging Interface, Multi-supplier Quote Requests (NO 3 and 4); Talk to sales, not only AI, easy to talk to human when needed (SE 1). Both markets, independently, asked for a human.

Probe"When did you last contact anyone here — advisor, supplier, support? What were you trying to do, and why didn't you just do it yourself?" This pain sits directly across from the sprint's AI premise. Do not soften it if it shows up.

P5

"Buying through you is more work than buying the way I already do."

Who · Buyer Markets · SE + NO Evidence: medium

Behind: Online Store & ERP Integration, Procurement Shopping Interface, Document Access, travel codes (NO 4); Nice upfront ERP integration possibility, onboarding with integrations (SE 3).

Probe"Walk me through the last order, step by step, from the moment you knew you needed it." Count the steps yourself. Then: "What would you have had to do differently to get the agreement price?" If the honest answer is "log in somewhere first", that is the friction, and no notification survives it.

P6

"The portal doesn't match the shape my company is actually in."

Who · Signer, larger SMBs Markets · NO-heavy Evidence: medium

Behind: Multi-company Agreement Support, Parent/Child Company Visibility, usage by location, team-specific filters, role-based access, multi-company account switching — all Norwegian, and a strong enough cluster that it's either a real market difference or one very vocal customer with subsidiaries. Worth knowing which.

Probe"How many sites or companies do you buy for? Who else has a login, and what can they see?" Check against account data before the interview so you know whether you're talking to a group or a single entity.

P7

"I have to document our suppliers, and I can't."

Who · Signer / compliance Markets · NO only Evidence: medium

Behind: Supplier Certifications & Transparency Docs, Transparency Act compliance documents, Green & Sustainable Agreement Highlighting (NO). Åpenhetsloven is Norwegian law with real reporting duties, which makes this the only pain on the board with a legal deadline behind it rather than a preference.

Probe"Does anyone ask you to document where you buy from? Who, how often, and what do you send them?" A genuinely different wedge — it isn't a savings story at all, and it may be the strongest reason a Norwegian customer would log in unprompted.

P8

"I forget you exist until the invoice reminds me."

Who · Buyer Markets · SE + NO Evidence: thin, many features

Behind: New & Expiring Agreement Notifications (NO 2); "Action required" banner, trigger email with CTA, notifications on a channel the CFO prefers, adjustment of the level of "sound" from the system (SE 4). Six proposed solutions, no observed behaviour.

Probe"When did you last open the portal, and what made you open it that day?" Then the hard one: "What else was in your inbox that morning, and what did you do with it?"
This is the sprint's premise and it has the thinnest support on either board. That doesn't make it wrong — it makes it the thing most worth checking before three days of prototyping rest on it.

What the shape of this map is telling you

The three strongest pains — P1, P2, P3 — all describe the same underlying failure from different angles: the service is bought once, at the top of the company, and then never reaches the people whose ordering habits it was supposed to change. The Signer can't see value because the Buyer never changed behaviour; the Buyer never changed behaviour because nobody ever handed them the agreements.

If the interviews confirm that, it reframes the sprint. The question stops being "how do we make the portal smarter" and becomes "how does an agreement reach the hands of the person who buys" — which is a distribution problem, and may well have an answer that is neither AI nor a dashboard.

From pains to questions

One How Might We per pain — provisional, and deliberately so

These are drafts written from reconstructed assumptions, not findings. Their job right now is to test the pain map: a pain that won't phrase into a decent HMW usually isn't sharp enough yet, and you can see that below in P6 and P8. Their second job is to give you something strong in your pocket when the facilitators run the HMW exercise on Day 1 — arriving with drafts beats generating from scratch in a room. Every one of them should be rewritten after the interviews. If they survive unchanged, the research didn't do its job.

Altitude — the thing most HMWs get wrong

Too high: "How might we make procurement easier for SMBs?" No traction — everything is an answer, so nothing is.

Too low: "How might we use AI to suggest deals in the dashboard?" That's a solution wearing a question mark. It hands the room a conclusion and calls it a brief.

Right: names a person, a moment, and the change — and stops before naming the mechanism. If you can't sketch three genuinely different answers to it, the altitude is wrong.

PainHow might we…
P1 · Handoff…get an agreement into the hands of the person who actually places the orders, without the person who signed having to teach them?
P2 · Proof of value…let the person paying for this see what they got for it, without having to go looking for it?
P3 · Discovery…make the right agreement obvious at the moment someone is choosing a supplier, without them having to know the catalogue?
P4 · Wants a human…give someone the confidence of talking to a person, at the speed of a system?
P5 · Friction…make buying through an agreement the path of least resistance rather than a detour?
P6 · Company shape…let a company with several sites see and steer what all of them are buying?
Weak — reads as a feature request because the pain underneath it isn't yet articulated. A sign P6 needs a real interview before it earns sprint time.
P7 · Compliance…turn supplier documentation from something you chase into a by-product of buying?
P8 · Forgetting us…reach the buyer at the moment they're deciding, instead of hoping they come to us?
Note what happens when you phrase it properly: it stops being about notifications and becomes about reach. Which is a much bigger, more interesting question than the six notification features on the boards.

Where they converge — the candidate sprint HMW

Three of the strongest — P1, P3 and P8 — collapse into one question once they're phrased as HMWs. All three are about the same missing thing: the agreement never reaches the person choosing, at the time they're choosing. If the interviews hold that up, this is the sentence I'd want on the wall:

How might we put the right agreement in front of the person doing the buying, at the moment they choose a supplier — without them having to know it exists?

It also happens to be where an automated system genuinely earns its place, which matters given the direction is fixed: answering it well requires knowing what a company buys, anticipating what's coming, and reaching outside the portal to a person who may never log in. That's a real job for AI rather than AI looking for one — and it's a far stronger brief than "suggest deals in the dashboard."

How to use these on Day 1

Don't table them at the start. Facilitators run HMW generation after the expert interviews on purpose — putting polished statements on the wall beforehand anchors the room to your framing and suppresses the notes everyone else is writing. Hold them, contribute yours into the same pile as everyone else's during clustering, and let them compete on merit. If they're good, they'll survive; if the interviews produced something sharper, you want the sharper one to win.

The constraint

AI is decided. Here's what that actually changes — and what it doesn't.

Worth being precise about this, because "the direction is set" gets used to shut down more than it should. A fixed mechanism is a normal design constraint. Every project has them: we build on the web, we use the design system, we ship in Q3. What it doesn't fix is which problem the mechanism is pointed at, and that's the entire question this research answers.

Don't bury P4

Both markets independently asked for a human — advisor chat, supplier messaging, "talk to sales, not only AI". With the AI direction now fixed there'll be a pull to treat that as noise. It isn't; it's a design constraint arriving early and cheap. The most likely successful shape here is AI that does the tedious work and hands off cleanly to a person at the moment of judgement — which is a better product than either alone, and you can only design it if you keep asking about the human handoff in every interview. Keep the P4 questions in the script exactly as written.

One practical consequence for the interviews: don't say the word "AI" to customers. It invites either enthusiasm or suspicion, and both are worthless as data. Ask what they do and where it hurts. You'll learn far more about where automation is welcome from someone describing a task they resent than from anyone reacting to the word.

Going first

You may be the one interviewing. Two decisions before you book anything.

Language

These are Norwegian SMB customers. If you don't run the interview in Norwegian, you will read second-language hesitation as confusion and hedging as doubt, and you'll be wrong. Options in order of preference: a Norwegian-speaking colleague moderates while you observe and own the analysis; or you moderate in English with a bilingual colleague in the room to catch what's underneath; or you moderate in English alone and treat everything about tone and hesitancy as unreliable. The third option still beats not doing the interviews — just know what you've given up.

Who you're allowed to talk to

Get the recruiting question settled early: can you contact customers directly, or does it route through account managers? If it routes through account managers, you will be handed happy customers, and happy customers confirm what you already believe. Push specifically for the two low-activity accounts and the one who never activated — and be ready for someone to be uncomfortable with that. Say plainly that you're not there to sell or to save the account, and mean it.

Rules of the room

Six habits that separate a useful interview from a pleasant chat

  • Ask about the last time, never about "usually". "Usually" produces a tidy story about how someone thinks they work. "The last time" produces what actually happened, including the parts that don't reflect well on anyone.
  • Never ask whether they'd use something. "Would you use a feature that…" is worth nothing. Nobody can predict their own behaviour, and everyone is polite. Ask what they did.
  • "Show me" beats "tell me". Screen share where you can. What people do and what they report doing come apart almost immediately, and the gap is where the design work lives.
  • Count to five after they finish. The first answer is the socially rehearsed one. The second answer, the one that arrives in the silence, is usually the true one. This feels unbearable and it works.
  • Follow the emotion, not the feature. Irritation, embarrassment, a small laugh, "well, to be honest…" — every one of those is a marker. Stop and dig there, even if it's off-script.
  • Use "tell me more about that" instead of a third "why". Two whys is curiosity. Three is an interrogation, and people start defending rather than describing.

The rule that keeps this honest — precision is brought, not asked

It's easy to write a script that says "find the pain" and then quietly asks people to audit their own account. "Which agreements have you never used?" · "Which of your last five orders could have gone through us?" · "How much did you save?" — all three demand an inventory nobody carries in their head, and all three force the person to think in our categories rather than their own day. You get a guess, delivered apologetically, and you've taught them the interview is a test.

Test every question against this: could they answer it from the memory of something that happened? If it needs a list, a total, or a category name, it's the wrong instrument. Three ways out, in order of preference:

  • Look it up beforehand. You have the transaction data. Arrive knowing which agreement went quiet in March, then use it to open a story: "I saw the workwear one stopped after March — what was going on around then?" Same precision, and it costs them nothing.
  • Ask for the shape instead of the inventory. Not "which agreements", but "was there something you set up and didn't carry on with?" People remember the abandoning, not the list.
  • Make it your homework, not theirs. Whether their last new supplier had an agreement available is an analysis you run afterwards. Ask them how they chose; work out the gap yourself.

And keep the instruments apart

Three different jobs kept sliding into one script: a discovery interview (why does this hurt?), a usability test (can you complete this task?), and data analysis (what does the account actually show?). Each needs the others, and each is ruined by being run inside the other. "Show me how you'd find a deal on office chairs" is a perfectly good usability task and a terrible pain question — it tests the portal you already have, on ground you already chose, in a conversation that was supposed to start outside the product. It's back in the kit as an optional observation block, clearly labelled, and it is the first thing to cut when time is short.

The specific trap in this project

These customers have already been asked for feedback once, and they answered in features. They'll do it again — they'll open with "you should add a comparison view." Don't write it down as a requirement and don't argue with it. Take it one layer down, every time, with two questions:

"What would that let you do that you can't do today?" and then "When did that last cost you something?"

If they can name the moment, you've found a pain. If they can't, you've found an idea — perfectly nice, but not evidence, and it goes on a different list.

The script

45 minutes, five blocks

Keep this on one page beside you, not on screen. The timings are a shape, not a schedule — if block three is going somewhere, stay in block three and cut block five.

00:00 – 03:00

Set the frame

Consent to record, and lower the stakes fast.

  • "I'm a designer, not a salesperson, and I didn't build the thing we're going to talk about — so be as blunt as you like."
  • "There are no wrong answers. I'm trying to understand your day, not test your knowledge."
  • "Is it OK if I record? It's only so I don't have to write while you talk."

03:00 – 08:00

Their world

Establish which of the three people you're actually sitting with.

  • "What does the company do, and what's your part in it?"
  • "What sorts of things get ordered here in a normal month?"
  • "Who else orders things? How do you divide it up?"Listen for whether they signed up or inherited it. That single fact reframes everything after it.

08:00 – 22:00

The last time ★ the core of the interview

One concrete purchase, reconstructed minute by minute. If you only get through this block, the interview was still worth doing.

  • "Think of the last thing you ordered for the company. Walk me through it from the moment you knew you needed it."Probe relentlessly: How did you know? What did you do first? Where were you — desk, phone, van? Did you check a price anywhere? Who else was involved? How long did the whole thing take?
  • "Now the last time you ordered from somewhere new — a supplier you hadn't used before. How did you end up choosing them?"This is where P3 lives. New-supplier decisions are where an agreement could have changed the outcome and usually didn't.
  • "Was there anything about that you'd have liked to go differently?"

22:00 – 32:00

Us, in their life

Still about events, not inventory. Nothing here asks them to recall a list or think in our categories.

  • "When did you last open it? What made you open it that day?"Tests P8 directly. "I can't remember" is a real and important answer — write it down verbatim.
  • "Can you show me? Just do whatever you'd normally do."Open-ended, no task set by you. Silence while they navigate; note every hesitation, back-click, and re-scan of the same area. If they say "I never really use it", don't push them into a demo — that answer is the finding.
  • "Was there something here you set up and then didn't carry on with?"P1. The shape, not the inventory. If you've done the homework you can name the specific one — "I saw the workwear agreement went quiet after March, what happened around then?" — which opens a story instead of demanding recall.

32:00 – 42:00

Value, and the gap

Find out whether the value is felt, proven, or merely assumed.

  • "Has anyone ever asked you to justify this? What did you show them?"P2. An event, not an estimate.
  • "Does anyone ask you to account for where the company buys from?"P7, and it doubles as the compliance probe in Norway. If the answer is yes, slow right down.
  • "If this disappeared tomorrow, what's the first thing you'd notice?"The best single question in the script. It separates value people feel from value they've been told about, and the answer is often uncomfortably small.

42:00 – 45:00

Close

Two questions that keep paying off.

  • "What haven't I asked about that I should have?"
  • "Is there someone else here who orders things, who'd see this differently from you?"This is how you reach the Buyer when you've been given the Signer.

The second instrument

Salespeople: a biased sensor, pointed somewhere the customer interviews can't reach

Two interview types, one map. It's worth being clear about what each one is for, because the most common way this goes wrong is treating salespeople as cheaper, more available customers. They aren't proxies. They're a different instrument with a different range.

The analytical trick — where the real pain hides

Sales tells you what was promised. Customers tell you what's actually used. The gap between those two is the most reliable pain-detector you have in this project, and it doesn't require either party to articulate a problem. When a salesperson says "I always tell them the coach will help them get set up" and no customer mentions a coach, you've found something neither of them would have told you directly.

So run at least two sales interviews before the customer ones, and carry their promises into the customer conversations as things to quietly check.

Two things never to ask a salesperson

"What should we build?" — you'll get their last three lost deals as a roadmap. Sincere, specific, and shaped entirely by their commission.

"What do customers want?" — you'll get feature language, same as the boards you already have. Ask instead what customers ask for that they can't answer, and what customers do after signing.

Sales script

The battery — 20 minutes, five blocks

Sales are here as witnesses to the customer. Every question is about the people they meet — what those people are trying to do and where it breaks — not about how they sell or how their week works. Your one job during the call is to keep dragging them from generalisation back to a named customer. Every time they say "customers usually…", ask "who, most recently?"

Twenty minutes means two things happen before the call

Pre-fill their patch from the CRM — market, segment, tenure, account count. Never spend live minutes on facts you can look up; at this length that's a sixth of the interview.

Send one line 24 hours ahead: "Before we talk — think of two customers you know really well, one who's thriving and one who isn't. I'll mostly be asking about them." They arrive with examples rather than generalities, which is the whole game at this length.

00:00 – 02:00

Frame it, and disarm the pitch reflex

They'll assume you're either evaluating them or taking requirements. Kill both in the first minute — at this length you can't afford a slow start.

  • "I want to borrow what you know about the customers. You talk to more of them in a week than I do in a quarter."
  • "Nothing here becomes a feature request, so you don't need to lobby for anything. And I'll keep asking you to name a specific customer — that's not doubt, it's just how I keep it concrete."

02:00 – 06:00

Who the customer actually is

Establish which of the three people they're describing, before anything else they say can be misread.

  • "Picture the customer you know best. Who are they — what's their job, and what are they trying to get done?"
  • "Who signs, and who in that company actually uses it day to day? Same person or not?"The single most valuable thing sales can tell you, and it targets P1 directly. If they don't know the answer, that is the finding — it means nobody is watching the handoff.

06:00 – 12:00

What customers struggle with ★ protect this block

The heart of it. Six minutes, and every general answer gets pulled back to a named case.

  • "What do customers struggle with most?"Then, every single time: "Who, most recently? Tell me about them." That one move is what turns a sales opinion into usable evidence, and it costs three seconds.
  • "What do they complain about after they've bought — not during the sale?"Post-sale complaints are the closest thing sales has to unfiltered user data. Nobody's being polite to close a deal any more.
  • "Which customers signed up and then never really got going? What's your read on why?"P1 again, from the other side. If they can name three off the top of their head, that's your customer interview shortlist — ask for the names now, while it's live.

12:00 – 17:00

The gap ★

Where a salesperson tells you something no dashboard will. Warm tone, and make clear you're not auditing them.

  • "What do customers ask you for that you can't give them a good answer to?"
  • "What do you tell them we'll do, that you slightly hold your breath about?"Every salesperson has one. Give it silence and it comes. Whatever they name is an expectation the product isn't meeting — and it arrives with no defensiveness because you framed it as a shared open secret rather than a failing.
  • "What do customers still want to phone a person about, rather than doing it themselves?"P4, from the other side. Especially worth having on record now that the AI direction is fixed.

17:00 – 20:00

Close, and recruit

Sales are your route to the customers you actually need — including the ones nobody will offer you.

  • "What do you think we in product don't understand about these customers?"Ask it straight. They've usually been waiting years for someone to ask, and the answer is often the best thirty seconds of the call.
  • "Which two customers should I talk to — including one who'd be blunt with me?"Say plainly you're not there to sell or to rescue the account. And be genuinely willing to accept a no: burning one rep's trust costs you the whole channel.

Swap-ins if you're given thirty minutes

In priority order: "When they say no to us, what are they saying yes to instead?" — status quo is usually the real competitor and it never appears on a battlecard · "What's going on inside a company when they're suddenly ready to buy?" — the trigger is the moment the product is genuinely wanted · "When anything automated has come up with a customer, how did they react?" · "If I sat in on your calls for a week, what would surprise me?"

What twenty minutes costs you

Worth naming so nobody is surprised later. You lose the deal walk-throughs — the closed one and the lost one — which is where the buying trigger and the real objection behind "too expensive" live. You'll get sales' summarised view of those instead of a reconstruction, and summaries are where bias concentrates.

Two mitigations: run four sales interviews rather than three, since each one is thinner and patterns need more repetitions to show up; and pull lost-deal reasons from the CRM beforehand so you can spend the live minutes on what the CRM can't tell you.

Coverage — who to run this on

Four to five salespeople, minimum one per market — a number up from three because twenty-minute interviews are thinner and patterns need more repetitions before you can trust them. Deliberately mix: one long-tenured (knows the history and the graveyard of past attempts), one recent joiner (still notices what's odd, hasn't yet learned to work around it), and one from each of NO and SE so you can tell a market difference from a personal style. At twenty minutes each, five of these cost you a single afternoon.

And one addition: run this on customer support or an advisor too. They're arguably the better sensor — they hear from customers after the sale, when there's nothing to gain by being polite, and they see the same failure fifty times. Roughly the same battery, with blocks 3 and 4 swapped for "what are the five things people contact you about most, and which one do you dread?"

After each one

Capture, and the thirty-minute rule

  • Record with consent; don't take heavy notes yourself. One line per notable moment with a timestamp is plenty. You cannot listen properly and transcribe at the same time, and listening is the job.
  • Write a one-page debrief within thirty minutes of hanging up — before the next interview, always. Top three surprises, three verbatim quotes, and which of P1–P8 moved. Memory of interview two contaminates interview one faster than anyone expects.
  • Track each pain as confirmed, killed, reshaped, or new — and mark which instrument moved it. A pain that both a salesperson and a customer independently point at is close to a fact. One that only sales sees is a hypothesis about customers, held by people who meet them in buying mode. The "new" column matters most of all: a pain nobody on either board predicted is the single most valuable thing you can carry into Day 1.
  • Keep a running list of promises. Every "I always tell them that…" from a sales interview goes on it, and every one gets quietly checked in the customer conversations. The unkept promises are your shortest route to a real pain.
  • Keep the verbatim quotes. On Day 1, one sentence in a customer's own words will outweigh an hour of internal debate. Have five ready.
