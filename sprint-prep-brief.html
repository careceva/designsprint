Where we are

What the first sprint actually produced

The AI Discovery & Prototyping workshop produced an affinity map: seven themes, twenty-eight features, all of them plausible. But read it closely and it answers only one question — what could we build? Every item is a solution. None of them is traced to something we watched a customer struggle with.

That is a normal outcome for an ideation workshop and not a failure. It just means the next step isn't more ideation. It's going back one layer and finding out which of those twenty-eight bets is standing on a real problem.

The good news: the affinity map is an excellent list of hypotheses. We already know what the team believes. Sprint 2 puts those beliefs under load.

The challenge

Send the facilitators a sharpened challenge before Day 1

Facilitators will run whatever statement they're handed. They don't know the product well enough to notice what's wrong with this one — you do. Getting a better version into their hands a week early is cheap; arguing about it at 09:30 on Tuesday in front of the whole team is expensive.

As written in the plan

How might we use AI to enhance purchasing business supplies in a proactive way for procurement responsibles to increase total everyday spend across more partner agreements?

Proposed for Sprint 2

How might we help the person who actually buys everyday supplies at a Nordic SMB get the agreement price without having to know which agreements exist?

Three things the original does to us:

  • It names the solution before the problem. "Use AI, proactively" is an answer. Bake it into the challenge and Day 1 becomes a search for justification rather than a search for truth. Keep AI as the leading hypothesis; don't make it the premise.
  • The outcome is our metric, not theirs. No office manager has ever wanted to increase total everyday spend across more partner agreements. They want the printer paper to arrive and not to get shouted at about the budget. Business value should follow user value in the sentence, not replace it.
  • "Procurement responsibles" is a job title, not a behaviour. In a twelve-person Swedish company, the person ordering coffee, paper and workwear is an office manager, a site foreman, or the owner's partner. Many of them have never used the word procurement. If we recruit by title we will test the wrong people.

The business framing stays on the wall alongside it — the sprint still has to move spend onto agreements. We just stop pretending that's what the user wants.

The bet that could sink everything

Central risk · test this first

Every one of the seven themes assumes the buying moment happens in our portal.

"Proactive AI" only works if there is a surface to be proactive on. But there is a real chance the buying moment lives somewhere we don't control: a reply to a supplier's email, a rep's phone number saved in someone's contacts, a webshop with saved card details, a line item entered straight into the ERP. If that's where the decision happens, then a smarter dashboard is a better answer to a question nobody asked.

So Day 1's single most valuable output is not a journey map of our portal. It's an honest answer to: where is the buyer standing at the moment they decide? If it isn't in the portal, the sprint pivots to reaching them there — email, Teams, the ERP, the supplier's own checkout — and that is a good outcome, not a derailment.

Hypotheses under load

The seven themes, and the pain each one is quietly assuming

Print this and put it on the wall. Every theme gets a probe on Day 1. Anything that survives is allowed into Day 2's sketching; anything that doesn't gets parked, loudly, so nobody smuggles it back in.

ThemeThe pain it assumesHow we check it on Day 1
1 · AI assistance & human support They can't find what they need and don't want to phone anyone. "Last time you needed something new — walk me through exactly what you did, minute by minute."
2 · Savings intelligence They don't believe or can't see the savings, so the value fades. "What did you save last year? Where would you go to find out? Does anyone ever ask you for that number?"

6 · Supplier & agreement management Agreements get added and then go stale, unused. "Which agreements do you actually buy from? Which did you add and never use — and why not?"
7 · Activation & conversion The path from signed-up to first purchase is too slow. Recruit one customer who never activated. Their answer is worth all six other rows.

Your single biggest lever

Week −1: do the research the sprint can't do, and walk in holding it

You asked the sprint to discover what actually hurts. Straight answer: a four-day sprint can't do generative research. Day 1 buys roughly three hours of expert time — enough to challenge a hypothesis, not to discover a pain cold. Run it unprepared and Day 1 becomes six well-informed people confidently sharing opinions, which is exactly how the last workshop produced an affinity map instead of a problem.

This is where being the in-house designer is an advantage rather than a constraint. External facilitators cannot pull transaction data, cannot get a churned customer on the phone, and cannot tell the difference between a real pain and a story the company tells itself. You can. Four to six customer interviews in the week before, 45 minutes each, recorded — roughly three days of your time.

Walk into Day 1 with that pain map and your position changes completely. You are no longer one voice with an opinion; you're the person holding the only evidence in the room. Hand it to the facilitators in advance and ask for a slot on Day 1 to present it — as raw material for the team to attack, not as a conclusion. If you do one thing from this document, do this.

Recruiting — from data, not from the sales team's favourites

17,000 customers and full transaction history is an unfair advantage; use it. Pull the sample deliberately:

  • 2 high-savings actives — what does "working" look like, and what do they do that others don't?
  • 2 low-activity accounts — agreements added, little or no spend against them. This is where the pain lives.
  • 1 never-activated or churned — the most uncomfortable half-hour of the sprint and the most useful.

Happy customers introduced by their account manager will confirm everything we already believe. Budget for the awkward ones.

Also worth fixing from last time: the previous sprint's expert interviews ran 20 minutes each and skewed internal — salespeople describing customer pain second-hand. This time, 30 minutes, and at least three of the four experts are people who personally place orders.

Craft decisions

Two prototype calls to win — you'll probably be the one building it

Both of these have to be argued for on Wednesday afternoon at the latest, and only one person in the room will be thinking about them. That's you.

Call one · build five data variants, not one demo

If the concept shows AI making suggestions, generic dummy data destroys the test. A procurement person can tell in two seconds whether "we noticed you buy a lot of coffee" is a real observation about their company or filler text. With fake data you learn whether the idea is believable. With their own data you learn whether it's useful — and only the second one tells you to build it.

So: pull each of the five testers' actual spend categories from transaction data and build five variants of the prototype. Roughly two extra hours on Day 3, and the highest-leverage two hours in the whole sprint. This needs a data access decision made this week, not on Thursday morning.

Call two · prototype the intervention, not the intelligence

The tempting thing to build is a clever recommendation engine. But the model's accuracy is an engineering question we can answer later and cheaply. The question we can only answer with a human in front of us is: does a busy office manager, mid-task, act on an unsolicited suggestion at all? Design the prototype around that moment — where it appears, what it interrupts, what it asks for — and let the suggestion itself be a fixed, well-chosen example.

Testing across three markets

Two SE, two NO, one DK — and what that costs us

  • Moderate in the participant's own language. Norwegian and Danish SMB buyers will underperform in English and we'll misread hesitation as confusion about the concept.
  • Prototype UI in English, and rule copy out of the findings. Building three localised variants isn't realistic in a day. The moderator flags it up front, and we agree in advance that no wording finding counts. What we're testing is comprehension of the concept, not the label.
  • Denmark goes last, at 14:25. DK is the newest and thinnest market — if the DK product differs from SE/NO, a DK tester can fail on things that have nothing to do with the concept. Running them fifth means we already hold four clean signals and can tell the two apart.
  • Brief the observers on what a market difference looks like versus a concept failure. Otherwise the loudest voice in the debrief attributes everything to "the Danes do it differently."

Your pre-sprint checklist

Six things to settle before Tuesday, and who to ask

  1. Make sure the facilitators know AI is already decided at C level.
    Answered · tell the facilitators before Day 1

    Settled: the AI direction is fixed above this sprint. Facilitators who don't know that will run a Day 1 that appears to put it up for debate, the room will spend two hours re-deciding something already decided, and everyone will leave irritated. Tell them in advance and reframe the day honestly: not whether AI, but which pain is severe enough that an automated intervention is welcome — and where it intervenes, and whether it acts, suggests, or hands off to a human. That's still a real sprint with a real open question at the centre of it.

  2. Who is the Decider?
    Ask: sprint sponsor · flag to facilitators once named

    Currently unnamed. Sofia Ekberg is the obvious candidate if she can commit all four days and has authority to fund what Friday recommends. A sprint without a Decider produces a recommendation nobody owns — and you'll be the one holding that recommendation in three months.

  3. Can I run customer interviews the week before?
    Ask: your manager · needs ~3 days of your time

    The Week −1 research above. This is your call to make and defend. Frame it as risk reduction, not extra research: without it, four days of six people's time rests on assumptions nobody checked. Get the recruiting brief out roughly two weeks ahead — SMB customers don't answer on 48 hours' notice.

  4. Can we put real customer transaction data in the prototype, and who signs it off?
    Ask: data / privacy owner · yes-or-no needed this week

    Blocks the five-variant prototype. Needs a name and an answer well before Thursday — a GDPR review on Thursday afternoon is not a plan, and the fallback (generic dummy data) meaningfully weakens what Friday can tell you.

  5. What am I building in, and is it ready?
    Ask: yourself · before Tuesday

    If the concept turns out conversational or data-driven, a clickable Figma file may not carry it, and Thursday is far too late to discover that. Have components, a starter file, and a plausible faster route ready to go. Thursday should be assembly, not construction.

  6. If Friday says the portal is the wrong surface, is there appetite to act on it?
    Ask: sponsor · privately, this week

    Now the sharpest question on the list, and the one to ask one-to-one rather than in a room. AI being decided doesn't settle where it lives — email, the ERP, a supplier's checkout, or a person's hands are all still on the table. But if the honest answer is "it has to be in the portal", then the sprint is scoped inside the portal whether anyone says so or not, and it's far better to know that now than to spend Friday afternoon arguing for a finding nobody was ever going to fund.
